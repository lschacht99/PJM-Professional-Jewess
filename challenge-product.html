The example problem

A better tool for a shared space.

Imagine a community center needs a portable, reusable display system: simple to assemble, compact to store, accessible to varied users, and inexpensive to fabricate.

Teams would develop an educational concept and demonstrate how they balanced weight, cost, ease of assembly, durability, and usability. The fictional brief avoids using participant work as unpaid commercial R&D.

Illustrative duration
Three weeks, mostly remote
Participant level
Beginners through early-career, with appropriate categories
Professional involvement
Brief review and one feedback session
Outputs
Sketches, optional CAD, evidence of testing, design rationale and 5-minute presentation
A sample learning sequence

Make it, test it, learn from it.

These stages are examples of the educational structure. A final challenge would be scoped with an advisor and reviewed before applications open.

WEEK 01

Frame the problem

Interview a hypothetical user, define success criteria and investigate existing solutions.

WEEK 02

Generate and test

Compare alternatives and test a low-risk model, simulation, or paper prototype.

WEEK 03

Communicate

Present evidence, decisions, constraints, and lessons learned.

REVIEW

Professional feedback

Receive constructive advice on feasibility, clarity, and next steps.

Educational standards

How we keep the experience useful and fair.

Participant-generated concepts remain educational. A real community-partner project would require a documented purpose, suitable supervision, IP terms, permissions, and labor-law review before acceptance.

01

Fair access

Low-cost participation and clear beginner-friendly entry points when feasible.

02

Meaningful feedback

Review should address what participants learned and why solutions work or fail.

03

Participant ownership

Any IP or showcase permissions must be transparent and appropriate.
